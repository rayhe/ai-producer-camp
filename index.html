Back the camp

Program proposal · Summer 2027 · Ages 10–14

AI can make almost anything. It can't check anything. We teach kids the second half.

Ground Truth Summer Camp is a two-week day camp where kids become producers for AI projects — the humans who collect real data, verify claims, build the physical thing, exercise judgment, and put their name on the result. Every day, campers do the exact work AI is demonstrably, almost magically bad at.

01 · Mission

Raise producers, not prompters.

The scarcest skill of the next decade isn't prompting an AI. It's knowing — with evidence — when the AI is wrong. Kids today grow up as native consumers of generated content. Nobody is teaching them to be the verifier, the builder, the person accountable for the output. That's this camp's entire reason to exist.

The thesis Generative AI can produce a plausible anything — a photo, a fact, a 3D model, a sympathetic sentence. What it cannot do is go outside, touch the object, interview the person, measure the claim, notice that it's wrong, or take responsibility when it is. Those aren't bugs to fix; they're the permanent human share of the work. We train kids for that share — on purpose, with real tools, for real stakes.

What a producer does

On a real AI project, the producer sets the goal, feeds the model real data, runs the quality gates, fixes what the machine gets wrong, and signs their name to the final product. It's the highest-leverage, least-automable role on the team. Our campers practice every piece of that job at kid scale: they direct AI tools constantly — but the deliverables only ship if they collected the data, checked the facts, tested the object, and can defend the result.

Why ages 10–14

This is the age when kids form their default relationship with technology. They're old enough to use real tools — cameras, calipers, 3D printers, soldering irons (supervised), interview recorders — and young enough that "the computer said so" hasn't yet hardened into an assumption. We catch them right before it does.

02 · Why now

The generation being raised on AI output has no training in AI's failure modes.

By 2026, school-age kids routinely meet generated text, images, and voices before they meet the verified kind. Meanwhile, every working professional knows the new bottleneck: generation is cheap, ground truth is expensive. The adults who thrive are the ones who can produce and verify. The kids who learn it at eleven will run circles around the ones who learn it at twenty-five.

03 · Curriculum

Ten days, two arcs: collect the truth, then produce with it.

Week 1 is Ground Truth — going into the field and the workshop to gather and check what AI can't. Week 2 is The Producer's Chair — directing AI tools on real projects and owning every result. Every day names the weakness it attacks, runs an AM and PM block, and ends with a shipped deliverable. AI tools are used daily — always as the assistant, never the author.

Week 1 · Ground Truth

Days 1–5 · the world AI can't touch
Day 1 · Monday

The Blind Taste Test

  • AM — The Gauntlet: campers judge 20 unlabeled artifacts (AI vs. human: photos, essays, poems, product designs) and vote on which is which. Reveal: even the machines' biggest fans were wrong half the time.
  • PM — Failure Autopsy: dissect real AI disasters — invented citations, six-fingered hands, confidently wrong history. Campers build the camp's shared rubric: how do we know something is true?
The weakness attackedAI has no idea when it's wrong. Neither, yet, do we. Today fixes the second part.
Deliverable · Personal "producer's checklist" + team truth-rubric poster hung on the lab wall
Day 2 · Tuesday

Data Safari

  • AM — Field Kit Issue: cameras, mics, clipboards. Teams get a mission (e.g., "document every plant species on the block") and shoot a labeled 150-photo dataset — angles, lighting, edge cases, the boring stuff models never see.
  • PM — Label Party: tag the morning's data, then ask an image model to identify the same plants. Grade it against the labels. Every miss goes on the Wall of Almost.
The weakness attackedAI only knows the world from data humans collected. Fresh, local, weird reality is invisible to it.
Deliverable · A cleaned, labeled, human-verified dataset (first entry in each camper's portfolio)
Day 3 · Wednesday

The Verification Bureau

  • AM — Claim Intake: campers receive a stack of AI-generated "facts" about their own town (population, history, who founded the school, what's at the corner of Main & 3rd). They rank confidence, then hit the field with measuring tape and questions.
  • PM — The Report: each team publishes a verdict per claim: verified / busted / unverifiable — with evidence: photos, measurements, a primary source. They learn the hardest phrase in the producer's vocabulary: "we couldn't confirm it."
The weakness attackedAI states invented specifics with total confidence and cannot check a single one.
Deliverable · Evidence-backed verification report on 10 AI claims, ≥3 field-measured
Day 4 · Thursday

Sensors & Signal

  • AM — Build the Instrument: solder-and-program a micro:bit weather/noise/light station (supervised, kid-safe). Sensors are how machines touch the world — campers build the touching part.
  • PM — Logging the Invisible: deploy stations around the venue; record real temperature, sound, and light data through the afternoon. Compare the day's actuals against an AI's confident morning "forecast." Discuss: why is fresh measured data worth more than a fluent prediction?
The weakness attackedAI has no senses. It can't take today's temperature, and it can't be trusted to guess it.
Deliverable · Working sensor station + overnight logged dataset graphed by hand (sensors deploy Day 4 PM; data harvested and graphed Day 5 AM before Fabrication begins)
Day 5 · Friday

Fabrication Friday

  • AM — Ask the Machine: campers prompt an AI to design a phone stand / pencil holder / hinge bracket, then print it as-is on the lab 3D printers. No edits allowed.
  • PM — Reality Check: test the prints: do they fit the phone? Survive a drop? Hold a door? Catalog the failures (tolerances, thin walls, fantasy geometry), then redesign by hand in CAD, reprint, and retest. The before/after pair goes in the portfolio.
The weakness attackedAI describes objects it has never held. Physics doesn't read the prompt.
Deliverable · Failed AI print + fixed human revision, presented side by side

Week 2 · The Producer's Chair

Days 6–10 · direct the machine, own the outcome
Day 6 · Monday

The Interview Room

  • AM — Question Craft: campers draft interview questions for a visiting guest (a veteran, a shop owner, a grandparent). Then test them twice: once asked by the camper, once asked by an AI chatbot. Compare what a real human actually tells you.
  • PM — The Recording: conduct and record 10-minute oral-history interviews in pairs; capture audio, transcribe, and mark the moments — hesitation, laughter, the follow-up that earned the story. AI can simulate an answer. It cannot earn one.
The weakness attackedAI's "empathy" is pattern-matched text. Trust is built in person, in a pause, with eye contact.
Deliverable · Edited 3-minute oral-history piece with the AI-vs-human question comparison
Day 7 · Tuesday

Robotics Gauntlet

  • AM — Program the Wanderer: teams code rover robots to navigate a real obstacle course (ramps, tunnels, a "swamp" of packing peanuts). Every plan meets friction, literally.
  • PM — The Gauntlet Runs: timed trials. When the rover flips or gets lost, the AI's suggested route and the actual course diverge — campers debug the physical world: wheel slip, sensor drift, tape that reflects wrong. Spatial truth beats simulation.
The weakness attackedAI reasons about space from text. It has never turned a corner, slipped on cardboard, or recovered from a flip.
Deliverable · Gauntlet completion video + debugging log of every real-world fix
Day 8 · Wednesday

The Taste Panel

  • AM — Curate the Show: the cohort faces the week's full pile of AI-assisted work and must choose what deserves the showcase wall. Campers run structured taste panels: score each piece against their Day 1 rubric, argue, defend, cut.
  • PM — Set the Bar: teams write actual QC gates for their capstones ("ships only if it passes X"). Guest judge (local maker/teacher) test-drives the rubric and tries to sneak bad work past it.
The weakness attackedAI averages all taste into mush. "Correct" is common; "good" is a judgment call someone has to make.
Deliverable · Written QC gate for each capstone + curated shortlist with panel scorecards
Day 9 · Thursday

Ship Day

  • AM — Produce: final capstone build sprints. Every team finishes a complete artifact — verified data story, printed object, interview piece, or rover demo — using AI where it helps and replacing it where it fails.
  • PM — Sign Your Name: each team publishes a producer's credit roll: what the AI did, what the humans did, what went wrong, and who fixed it. Accountability isn't a lecture — it's the last page of every project.
The weakness attackedNo model can be responsible for an outcome. Campers learn responsibility is the feature, not the tax.
Deliverable · Shipped capstone + signed producer's credit: humans, machine, failures, fixes
Day 10 · Friday

Showcase Night

  • AM — Gallery Build: campers install the exhibition: datasets, prints, sensor graphs, interview audio, the Wall of Almost, and the failed-print museum. Every display explains what the AI couldn't do and who did it instead.
  • PM — Parents' Night: families tour the gallery. Each camper stands at their station for 4 minutes: show the work, name the weakness, prove the check. The evening ends with the taste-panel awards and diplomas: Producer of AI Projects, Certified Human.
The weakness attackedAI can't explain itself to your parents. These kids can explain anything they shipped.
Deliverable · Public showcase + capstone portfolio taken home by every camper

05 · Capstone

Every camper leaves with proof they did what the machine couldn't.

The capstone isn't a diorama — it's a small, real production with an AI in the crew and a kid in charge. Teams of four pick one track and ship it at Showcase Night:

Track A · The Field Guide

Verified local dataset

A human-collected, human-verified dataset of the neighborhood (species, sounds, measurements) — with a model graded against it and every miss explained.

Track B · The Object

Fabricated thing

A physical product born from an AI design, rescued by human engineering: original prompt, failed print, fixed revision, load test on video.

Track C · The Story

Human-sourced piece

An oral-history audio piece no model could record — a real interview, edited, fact-checked, and published with a producer's credit roll.

Every capstone ships with the same final artifact: the credit roll — one page naming what the AI did, what the humans did, what failed, and who fixed it. Parents don't just see the project; they see their kid taking responsibility for a machine's work. That page is the diploma.

06 · Staff

Five humans AI can't replace, on purpose.

Roles first, names at hire. Each instructor owns one week-1 or week-2 spine and floats across both. Target: licensed educators and working makers with maker-space or lab experience; every hire background-checked, first-aid certified, and trained on our supervision and tool-safety protocols.

Age-differentiation model (10–14 range): The four-year spread within the cohort is bridged through tiered challenge variants, not separate tracks. Each day's mission ships with a base version (accessible at 10) and an extension layer (expected at 13–14): e.g., Day 2's photo dataset uses GPS tagging for juniors and adds metadata schema design for seniors; Day 5's CAD redesign uses guided templates for 10-year-olds and freeform modeling for 14-year-olds; Day 7's rover gauntlet adds autonomous waypoint navigation for the older cohort. CITs (16+) provide peer scaffolding for younger campers. Kids who already code get harder variants of every mission — the rubric scales, the thesis doesn't.

Camp Director / Lead Producer

Runs the whole production

Experienced educator or informal-education director. Owns safety, families, schedule, and the daily "AI Fail of the Day." The accountable adult — modeling the camp's core value at org scale.

Data Naturalist

Owns Days 2–4

Field-biology or data background. Leads data safaris, sensor builds, and the Verification Bureau. Teaches kids that a measurement beats an opinion, and an evidence trail beats both.

Maker-in-Residence

Owns Days 5 & 9

Working fabricator / shop teacher. Runs CAD, printers, and the failed-print museum. Teaches tolerances, test protocols, and the pride of fixing what a machine flubbed.

Story & Judgment Lead

Owns Days 1, 6 & 8

Journalist, librarian, or documentary background. Runs taste panels, interview training, and the verification rubric. Teaches the difference between fluent and true.

Robotics & Wiring Lead

Owns Day 7 + tech ops

Robotics-club coach or engineer. Runs the gauntlet, keeps the fleet alive, and manages the camp's device/laptop cart and AI tool accounts.

CIT crew · 4 teens

Alumni-style counselors-in-training

Local high schoolers (16+) who manage materials, buddy-ratios on field missions, and the Wall of Almost. A ladder: campers at 12, CITs at 16, instructors at 22.

10 · Execution plan

Launching without humans (mostly).

This camp can be built by one operator + a stack of real APIs, with humans inserted exactly where the law, insurers, and child safety demand it. Below: the full launch plan — seven phases, T-minus timeline, itemized budget, kill gates, and the honest list of steps that cannot be human-free. Every named API was verified to exist in Aug 2026; every marketplace that doesn't expose an API is flagged and given a workaround.

The operating thesis APIs do the paperwork: form the entity, bind insurance, run background checks, push ads, collect tuition, text parents, track rosters. Humans own the judgment: permit walk-throughs, insurance riders, adjudicating a flagged background check, and — non-negotiably — supervising children. Roughly 90% of the launch tasks run without a human touching them; the remaining 10% is the part that makes it legal and safe, compressed into seven single-decision human gates.

Phase 1 · T-20 → T-16 weeks (Jan–Feb)

Compliance & insurance

~4 weeks · ~$4,100

This plan complies with all three. human seam: county inspector

  • Thimble API (thimble.com/api) — binds base General Liability by API, by the job, month, or year, and issues the certificate of insurance naming the venue host as additional insured — which is what actually unblocks the venue booking. verified API
  • Honest seam: the abuse & molestation endorsement — which county inspectors and sane parents require — is underwritten by specialists (camp insurers like K&K), not purchasable via any self-serve API. One broker call. ~$1,200/yr.
  • Checkr API — invite + run 8 adult background checks (5 staff incl. the director + 3 bench candidates) at ~$65 avg incl. county fees. The 4 CITs are minors — FCRA checks on 16-year-olds are legally fraught and mostly meaningless; they get reference checks, training, and supervised roles instead. verified API
  • AB 506 / mandated-reporter training — online, ~$20/head for all 12, certificates auto-filed to the compliance folder.

Honest seams — the humans this plan refuses to remove

Each seam is compressed to a single human decision, made once (or once a day), with everything else prepared by the stack:

  • 1 · County health inspector — the organized-camp permit requires a human walk-through and sign-off at the venue. One meeting, T-2 weeks.
  • 2 · Insurance broker — the abuse & molestation endorsement isn't self-serve on any API. One call, T-20 weeks.
  • 3 · Background-check adjudication — FCRA adverse-action review of any flagged candidate. A human reads, a human decides, per candidate.
  • 4 · Venue deposit approval — one decision on a shortlist of six. Money moves only after the click.
  • 5 · The adults in the room — 9 background-checked, trained humans on site every single day. This isn't overhead; it's the product.
  • 6 · Medication & incident command — the director administers meds and owns every incident call. Never automatable, never delegated to software.
  • 7 · Pickup authorization — photo-ID match against the signed authorization record, at the door, every day. The robot never dismisses a child.

11 · FAQ

The questions parents actually ask.

Does my kid need AI or coding experience?

None. We assume zero on Day 1 and teach every tool from the button up. The camp's core skills — observing, checking, building, asking, deciding — need no prerequisites. Kids who already code get harder variants of every mission.

Wait — is this two weeks of screens?

The opposite. Roughly half of every day is outside or at a workbench: walking the block with a camera, soldering a sensor, testing prints, flying rovers through an obstacle course. AI tools appear constantly, but as the thing being directed, checked, and out-performed — not the destination.

Soldering irons and 3D printers at age 10?

Every tool is introduced with a supervised demonstration and a safety gate a camper must pass before solo use (always within arm's reach of an instructor). Heated tools run at supervised stations with ventilation and PPE. Our Robotics & Wiring Lead owns tool safety; the ratio never drops below 1:6.

Which AI tools do you use, and are they safe for kids?

School-managed accounts on mainstream, kid-safe AI tools (image generation, chat, CAD assistants) with content filtering on, no personal data entered, and strict "we don't feed it our faces or friends' names" rules taught on Day 2. Accounts are camp-owned and wiped at session end.

What does my kid actually take home?

A producer's portfolio: their verified dataset, the failed print and its human-fixed twin, sensor graphs, interview audio, the debugging log, and the signed credit roll — plus a diploma reading Producer of AI Projects, Certified Human.

Is there financial aid?

Yes — a donor-funded scholarship bench covers 20% of seats (5 of 24), prioritized through local school partners. Application is one short form; decisions are need-blind and made before enrollment closes.

Why "producers" and not "prompt engineers"?

Prompting is a technique; producing is a job. A producer owns the outcome: the data, the checks, the fixes, and the blame when it's wrong. Prompting is one arrow in that quiver — we teach all ten.

What if AI gets better at all this by 2027?

Then the curriculum sharpens — the weakness list is re-audited each spring and days are rebuilt around whatever AI is currently worst at. The mission outlives any specific failure mode: the human who verifies, builds, and takes responsibility stays essential. (Also: we'll believe the demo when we can check it. That's the whole camp.)
