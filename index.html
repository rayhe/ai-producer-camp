Program proposal · Summer 2027 · Ages 10–14

AI can make almost anything. It can't check anything. We teach kids the second half.

Ground Truth Summer Camp is a two-week day camp where kids become producers for AI projects, the humans who collect real data, verify claims, build the physical thing, exercise judgment, and put their name on the result. Every day, campers do the exact work AI is demonstrably, almost magically bad at.

01 · Mission

Raise producers, not prompters.

The scarcest skill of the next decade isn't prompting an AI. It's knowing, with evidence, when the AI is wrong. Kids today grow up as native consumers of generated content. Nobody is teaching them to be the verifier, the builder, the person accountable for the output. That's this camp's entire reason to exist.

The thesis Generative AI can produce a plausible anything, a photo, a fact, a 3D model, a sympathetic sentence. What it cannot do is go outside, touch the object, interview the person, measure the claim, notice that it's wrong, or take responsibility when it is. Those aren't bugs to fix; they're the permanent human share of the work. We train kids for that share, on purpose, with real tools, for real stakes.

What a producer does

On a real AI project, the producer sets the goal, feeds the model real data, runs the quality gates, fixes what the machine gets wrong, and signs their name to the final product. It's the highest-leverage, least-automable role on the team. Our campers practice every piece of that job at kid scale: they direct AI tools constantly, but the deliverables only ship if they collected the data, checked the facts, tested the object, and can defend the result.

Why ages 10–14

This is the age when kids form their default relationship with technology. They're old enough to use real tools, cameras, calipers, 3D printers, soldering irons (supervised), interview recorders, and young enough that "the computer said so" hasn't yet hardened into an assumption. We catch them right before it does.

02 · Why now

The generation being raised on AI output has no training in AI's failure modes.

By 2026, school-age kids routinely meet generated text, images, and voices before they meet the verified kind. Meanwhile, every working professional knows the new bottleneck: generation is cheap, ground truth is expensive. The adults who thrive are the ones who can produce and verify. The kids who learn it at eleven will run circles around the ones who learn it at twenty-five.

03 · Curriculum

Ten days, two arcs: collect the truth, then produce with it.

Week 1 is Ground Truth, going into the field and the workshop to gather and check what AI can't. Week 2 is The Producer's Chair, directing AI tools on real projects and owning every result. Every day names the weakness it attacks, runs a 60-minute instructor-led lesson with a minute-by-minute run-of-show, and continues into a 45-minute self-directed project block with a printed handout. AI tools are used daily, always as the assistant, never the author.

Week 1 · Ground Truth

Days 1-5 · the world AI can't touch

Each day is one 60-minute instructor-led lesson with a minute-by-minute run-of-show, followed by a 45-minute self-directed project block campers complete from a printed handout, in the same room. The block needs no adult instruction: the handout, the answer key, and the wall rubric do the teaching, and the instructor stays in the room, silent, supervising. Every day also lists its exact materials and a prep checklist completable by one person in 30 minutes.

Day 3 · Wednesday

The Verification Bureau

0:00-0:10Claim intake. Each team gets a stack of 5 AI-generated "facts" about the town (population, founding year, what's at Main and 3rd). Campers rank their confidence on the claim sheet and write one line: "how I'd check it."
0:10-0:40Verification sprint. Single-group walk to two scripted stops (a shop owner briefed the week before, a public plaque). All three teams work in parallel at each stop: eight minutes per stop, two stops, sixteen minutes of field time. Measuring tape out, questions asked, photos taken; every team stays inside the group and the instructor runs the clock. The rest of the thirty minutes is about ten minutes of walking between stops plus slack. The clock time is eight minutes per stop, never eight minutes per team.
0:40-0:55Verdicts. Back inside. Teams fill verdict cards: verified, busted, or unverifiable, with evidence attached (photo, measurement, primary source). The instructor demos citing a primary source in one minute; each team reads one verdict aloud. The phrase of the day: "we couldn't confirm it."
0:55-1:00Handoff. Remaining-claims handout (finish with a parent or in the project block). Crew collects the 3 cameras back to the labeled charging tray on the equipment shelf, plus the tapes and clipboards.
If it runs longIf the walk runs past 0:40, the instructor demos one verdict aloud and each team finishes two cards in the room. The remaining claims move to the project block; the answer key is the pre-verification sheet.

Day 6 · Monday

The Interview Room

0:00-0:10Open. AI Fail of the Day, then the mission brief: "Today you learn the question a machine can never ask." Question-craft sheets issued; the visiting guest is introduced, a community member briefed in advance, present for the Day 6 lesson, never alone with a camper.
0:10-0:35Question craft. Campers draft 8 interview questions on the sheet and test them with the AI chatbot on a laptop. Then three campers each put one question to the guest live, and the class compares what a real human actually said versus what the chatbot invented. Juniors write fact-finding questions; seniors write questions that earn a story.
0:35-0:55Recording rotation. Two waves, 8 minutes each. Wave 1: pairs A, B, and C record their 8-minute interviews, A with the guest at the center table, B and C running peer interviews from their question-craft sheets in two corners. D, E, and F sit in the observation arc around the guest table, each with a Moment Marker card, live-marking the three marker moments (the correction, the laugh, the follow-up that earned the story) as A's guest interview unfolds. Then they swap: D interviews the guest in wave 2 while E and F record peer interviews, and A, B, and C take the observation arc to mark D's guest interview live. Wave 1 records on interview recorders 1 through 3 and wave 2 on recorders 4 through 6 (the 3 field-kit recorders plus the 3 extra recorder kits), one recorder per pair for the whole session, so each recorder holds exactly one 8-minute interview and every pair keeps its own recorder into the project block. The guest can only sit for two interviews, so the other four pairs conduct peer interviews; every pair records a full 8 minutes, and the two guest interviews go into the shared album for the showcase. The instructor circulates the whole time; all recording happens inside the room. Each camper keeps the same Moment Marker card for the project block.
0:55-1:00Handoff. "Moment Marker" transcription handout issued. Each pair keeps its recorder through the project block for the listen-back. At block end, crew collects the 6 recorders, their splitters, and the earbud pouches to the labeled charging tray on the equipment shelf, and the instructor confirms all six charge lights before pickup pings. No recorder ever leaves the room. The guest is thanked at the room door and shown out; the instructor stays in the room with the twelve campers, because the safety plan's line-of-sight rule has no exceptions.
If it runs longSix recorders, one per pair, 8-minute interviews: first wave 8 minutes, a 2-minute swap, second wave 8 minutes, 2 minutes of slack. The swap is the only moving part: the next three pairs are at their stations (two corners plus the guest table) and the next observation arc is seated before the last three leave. If the guest is late, pair A does a peer interview in wave 1; the wave-1 observation arc rehearses its live-marking on that peer interview until the guest arrives, the guest sits with the next recording pair when they arrive, and the second guest interview moves to the top of the project block, led by the Moment Marker handout. The guest departs through the room door right after that interview, the instructor staying in the room.

Day 8 · Wednesday

The Taste Panel

0:00-0:10Open. Revisit the Day 1 truth rubric, now with seven days of evidence behind it. The brief: the cohort's full pile of work goes on the wall; only what passes the panel earns a showcase slot.
0:10-0:40Taste panel. One panel, all twelve: every camper puts their single best piece on the wall and gets 2.5 minutes, score, argue, defend, cut, twelve pieces in thirty minutes, the clock is the discipline. The instructor moderates the room from the front; no piece is discussed in a corner out of sight. Juniors read the scores aloud; seniors argue the counter-case. The visiting guest judge watches all twelve pieces and picks the weakest survivor; the judge's gambit runs in the first five minutes of the next block. If the panel holds, the rubric is real.
0:40-0:55The judge's gambit + QC gates. Fifteen minutes, in three fives. 0:40-0:45: the guest judge argues the weakest survivor should not have made the cut; the panel defends or concedes, and the verdict is recorded beside the piece. 0:45-0:50: each capstone team drafts its actual gate, "ships only if it passes X." 0:50-0:55: the class stress-tests two of the drafts aloud, 2.5 minutes each. The full gate writing happens in the project block; what leaves this block is the draft, the verdict, and the stress-test memory.
0:55-1:00Handoff. QC-gate page template issued. The guest judge is thanked at the room door and shown out; the instructor stays in the room with the twelve campers, because the safety plan's line-of-sight rule has no exceptions.
If it runs longIf the panel deadlocks on a piece, the instructor calls the cut at the 2.5-minute mark and the piece goes on the Wall of Almost with the guest judge's verdict written beside it. The one-adult safety plan, in full.

Every procedure below is engineered for one adult and twelve campers, with no second adult at any moment. When a procedure cannot be done safely with one adult, the procedure is changed, never the staffing assumption.

  • Supervision modelThe instructor is in line-of-sight of all 12 campers every minute of the 60-minute lesson. Seating is a U around the work tables so no camper is behind the instructor. The 45-minute project block happens in the same room or at home, never in a second room that needs its own adult. Pickup: a camper leaves only with a person on the Airtable pickup-authorization list from enrollment; any change requires the parent to message the camp number, never a verbal relay through the child. Restroom: single-use restroom next to the main room; one camper at a time, the door visible from the instructor's seat, with a buddy waiting at the doorway inside the room. Project block: the instructor stays in the room the whole 45 minutes doing silent admin (attendance, incident log, tomorrow's kit staging), no instruction, no minute unsupervised.
  • Soldering station (one, instructor-run)The solder station runs only while the instructor stands at it, hands on. The instructor is the only person who touches the hot iron; campers position pre-tinned wires and read the meter while the instructor makes the joint. Pairs rotate 5 minutes each, timed, the queue posted on the wall; six pairs fill the 30-minute build block exactly, matching the Day 4 run-of-show. Everyone else does non-heated work (wiring diagrams, code) at the tables. PPE: safety glasses on every face within arm's length, fume extractor on, burn kit within 10 feet. When the instructor steps away from the station, even one step, the iron goes back in the stand and the power switch goes off. At close, the station is locked in the kit box, cord wrapped, before the project block starts.
  • Cutting toolsOne cutting station, one camper at a time, one instructor within arm's reach. Craft knives: retractable, cut-away-from-body rule, metal ruler, self-healing mat. The instructor pre-cuts any difficult stock during the 30-minute prep block so the camper cut is always the easy one. Scissors for precision paper work sit at the tables freely; nothing else does. No camper carries an open blade across the room: blade is retracted, handed to the instructor, instructor carries it.
  • 3D printers (two)Both printers are enclosed models; no hot-end contact is possible with the enclosure closed, and the enclosure stays closed while printing. The instructor loads filament and starts every print; campers submit files to a print queue during the project block, never touch the machine mid-print. Cooldown: parts come off the bed with a scraper tool, after the bed reads under 50C on the display, shown to the camper as the readout. The instructor removes the first hot part of the day as the demonstration.
  • Field missions (single group, no splits)Field missions move as one group of 12 plus the instructor, never in buddy pairs or sub-teams out of sight. The mapped route is shared with parents in the enrollment packet: no unmarked street crossings, no water, no entering buildings. Buddy pairs exist for accountability (your buddy checks you are present), not for separation: a buddy pair never leaves the instructor's sightline. Count in and count out, aloud, at every transition. Tier 2 (beyond a bandaid: burn, deeper cut, twisted ankle, allergic reaction without airway involvement): first aid from the instructor's kit, parent pinged via Twilio within 15 minutes, incident report form filled the same hour. Tier 3 (head injury, suspected concussion, airway involvement, lost camper): 911 first, then the parent call, then the incident form. Every incident, including Tier 1, gets a timestamped log entry; the log is exported to the parent at session end. The camp's one-adult design means no one chases a runaway while 11 wait: a camper who leaves the room triggers Tier 3 immediately, the instructor calls for help and counts the group.
  • Emergency: fire, lockdown, lost camperThe venue's evacuation plan is walked with the instructor at the T-2 site visit and rehearsed with the campers in the first 10 minutes of Day 1: assembly point, headcount there, no re-entry. Lockdown: doors lock from inside, campers to the far wall, attendance called silently, Twilio message to parents queued but not sent until the all-clear (no panicked texts mid-incident). Lost camper drill: the group freezes, the instructor counts, the venue is searched inward from the last known position; if the camper is not found in 5 minutes, it becomes a Tier 3 call.

One adult, two named responsibilities. The incident log, the med log, and the attendance record are camp property, exported to the parent at session end, and kept on file for three years. 06 · Staff

One instructor, by design.

The whole program is engineered so a single adult can run it: twelve campers, sixty minutes a day, every material pre-kitted, every post-lesson project block self-directed. Age-differentiation model (10–14 range): The four-year spread within the cohort is bridged through tiered challenge variants, not separate tracks. 12 · FAQ

The questions parents actually ask.

Does my kid need AI or coding experience?

None. We assume zero on Day 1 and teach every tool from the button up. The camp's core skills, observing, checking, building, asking, deciding, need no prerequisites. Kids who already code get harder variants of every mission.

Wait, is this two weeks of screens?

The opposite. Roughly half of every day is outside or at a workbench: walking the block with a camera, soldering a sensor, testing prints, driving rovers through an obstacle course. AI tools appear constantly, but as the thing being directed, checked, and out-performed, not the destination.

Soldering irons and 3D printers at age 10?

Every tool is introduced with a supervised demonstration and a safety gate a camper must pass before solo use, always within arm's reach of the instructor. Heated tools run at supervised stations with ventilation and PPE. With one adult and twelve campers, tool use is station-gated: only one heated station runs at a time, the rest of the group works on non-tool tasks. Our instructor owns tool safety personally.

Which AI tools do you use, and are they safe for kids?

School-managed accounts on mainstream, kid-safe AI tools (image generation, chat, CAD assistants) with content filtering on, no personal data entered, and strict "we don't feed it our faces or friends' names" rules taught on Day 2. Accounts are camp-owned and wiped at session end.

What does my kid actually take home?

A producer's portfolio: their verified dataset, the failed print and its human-fixed twin, sensor graphs, the interview transcript (three hand-transcribed marker moments from the Moment Marker handout; the interview audio itself stays on the camp's recorders, which never leave the room), the debugging log, and the signed credit roll, plus a diploma reading Producer of AI Projects, Certified Human.

Is there a device-free option for take-home work?

Yes, and it is the default, not the exception. Every take-home item has a paper path: each day's project-block handout carries a perforated dinner-table conversation card as a tear-off strip (the ten questions are printed in the handout kit spec in §03b, one per day), the journal is a physical notebook, and anything that uploads to the camp gallery has a paper alternative (bring the journal in; at pickup the instructor runs the flagged pages through the flatbed scanner on the door check-in table, about 30 seconds a page, and each scan lands in that camper's QR-linked gallery). Campers never take a shared device home; nothing that leaves the room needs a charger. Each day the camper tears off the conversation card at pickup, a single question for the dinner table, like "Ask a grown-up: when has a computer been confidently wrong in your job?" No screen, no app, no login required for any of it.

Is there financial aid?

Yes, a donor-funded scholarship bench covers 2 of 12 seats per session, prioritized through local school partners. Application is one short form; decisions are need-blind and made before enrollment closes.

Why "producers" and not "prompt engineers"?

Prompting is a technique; producing is a job. A producer owns the outcome: the data, the checks, the fixes, and the blame when it's wrong. Prompting is one arrow in that quiver, we teach all ten.

What if AI gets better at all this by 2027?

Then the curriculum sharpens, the weakness list is re-audited each spring and days are rebuilt around whatever AI is currently worst at. The mission outlives any specific failure mode: the human who verifies, builds, and takes responsibility stays essential. (Also: we'll believe the demo when we can check it. That's the whole camp.)
