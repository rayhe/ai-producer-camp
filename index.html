Back the camp

Program proposal · Summer 2027 · Ages 10–14

AI can make almost anything. It can't check anything. We teach kids the second half.

Ground Truth Summer Camp is a two-week day camp where kids become producers for AI projects, the humans who collect real data, verify claims, build the physical thing, exercise judgment, and put their name on the result. Every day, campers do the exact work AI is demonstrably, almost magically bad at.

01 · Mission

Raise producers, not prompters.

The scarcest skill of the next decade isn't prompting an AI. It's knowing, with evidence, when the AI is wrong. Kids today grow up as native consumers of generated content. Nobody is teaching them to be the verifier, the builder, the person accountable for the output. That's this camp's entire reason to exist.

The thesis Generative AI can produce a plausible anything, a photo, a fact, a 3D model, a sympathetic sentence. What it cannot do is go outside, touch the object, interview the person, measure the claim, notice that it's wrong, or take responsibility when it is. Those aren't bugs to fix; they're the permanent human share of the work. We train kids for that share, on purpose, with real tools, for real stakes.

What a producer does

On a real AI project, the producer sets the goal, feeds the model real data, runs the quality gates, fixes what the machine gets wrong, and signs their name to the final product. It's the highest-leverage, least-automable role on the team. Our campers practice every piece of that job at kid scale: they direct AI tools constantly, but the deliverables only ship if they collected the data, checked the facts, tested the object, and can defend the result.

Why ages 10–14

This is the age when kids form their default relationship with technology. They're old enough to use real tools, cameras, calipers, 3D printers, soldering irons (supervised), interview recorders, and young enough that "the computer said so" hasn't yet hardened into an assumption. We catch them right before it does.

02 · Why now

The generation being raised on AI output has no training in AI's failure modes.

By 2026, school-age kids routinely meet generated text, images, and voices before they meet the verified kind. Meanwhile, every working professional knows the new bottleneck: generation is cheap, ground truth is expensive. The adults who thrive are the ones who can produce and verify. The kids who learn it at eleven will run circles around the ones who learn it at twenty-five.

03 · Curriculum

Ten days, two arcs: collect the truth, then produce with it.

Week 1 is Ground Truth, going into the field and the workshop to gather and check what AI can't. Week 2 is The Producer's Chair, directing AI tools on real projects and owning every result. Every day names the weakness it attacks, runs a 60-minute instructor-led lesson with a minute-by-minute run-of-show, and continues into a 45-minute self-directed project block with a printed handout. AI tools are used daily, always as the assistant, never the author.

Week 1 · Ground Truth

Days 1-5 · the world AI can't touch

Each day is one 60-minute instructor-led lesson with a minute-by-minute run-of-show, followed by a 45-minute self-directed project block campers complete from a printed handout. The project blocks need no adult: the handout, the answer key, and the wall rubric do the supervising. Every day also lists its exact materials and a prep checklist completable by one person in 30 minutes.

Day 1 · Monday

The Blind Taste Test

0:00-0:10Open. Welcome, camp rules in three minutes, then the "AI Fail of the Day": one real, recent blunder dissected in five. Teams of four assigned; seats set for the session.
0:10-0:40The Gauntlet. Each team gets a tray of 20 unlabeled artifacts (photos, essays, poems, product designs; half AI-made, half human). Campers vote human-or-AI on answer cards. The instructor circulates and asks the dangerous question: "What made you sure?"
0:40-0:55Reveal + rubric build. Answers revealed team by team. The shock is the point: confident guesses land near 50%. The group writes the camp's shared rubric, "How do we know something is true?", on the poster that hangs on the lab wall all session.
0:55-1:00Handoff. Each camper gets a laminated Producer's Checklist and the project-block handout. Crew resets the trays.
Materials, 12 campers, pre-kitted
  • 5 artifact trays x 20 pre-printed, numbered cards (100 cards total)
  • 12 answer cards + 12 laminated producer checklists
  • Truth-rubric poster board + 4 markers
  • 1 pre-loaded "AI Fail of the Day" slide (instructor laptop)
Prep checklist, one person, about 25 min
  • Open kit box "Day 1"; lay out 5 trays on the tables
  • Place 12 answer cards and checklists at seats
  • Hang poster board; test the markers
  • Load the fail-of-the-day slide; confirm the projector
The weakness attackedAI has no idea when it's wrong. Neither, yet, do we. Today fixes the second part.
Deliverable · Personal producer's checklist + team truth-rubric poster on the lab wall
Project block (self-directed, 45 min) · Handout: "Failure Autopsy". Campers watch a 10-minute pre-recorded teardown of two real AI disasters, add three more examples they find themselves to their journals, and finish the checklist's Day 1 items. Answer key lives in the instructor folder; the rubric poster answers the hard questions.
Day 2 · Tuesday

Data Safari

0:00-0:10Open + kit issue. Cameras, lav mics, clipboards, mission cards. Mission brief: "document every plant species on the block." Rules: the cohort moves as one group, inside the chalk boundary, cameras always on straps. Juniors GPS-tag each photo; seniors add a metadata schema (species, angle, light, confidence).
0:10-0:40Data safari. One block, single group, instructor at the front. Each camper shoots at least 25 photos and logs angles, lighting, and edge cases on the tally sheet. The instructor calls out the boring shots models never see: undersides, damaged leaves, half-hidden stems.
0:40-0:55Label party. Back inside. Teams tag the morning's photos in the shared album, run an image model on 10 of their own plants, and grade it against their labels. Every miss goes on the Wall of Almost.
0:55-1:00Handoff. Labeling handout issued (20 photos each to finish). Crew collects cameras for charging.
Materials, 12 campers, pre-kitted
  • 3 field kits (camera, lav mic, clipboard, chalk, measuring card), one per team of 4
  • 12 tally sheets + 12 mission cards
  • Wall of Almost board + grading sheet ("AI vs. you")
  • 1 laptop at the grading station, image-model tab open, content filter on
Prep checklist, one person, about 25 min
  • Charge the 3 cameras; wipe memory cards
  • Load tally sheets onto the clipboards; stack mission cards
  • Set the grading station; test the model tab and filter
  • Walk the block once and lay the chalk boundary
The weakness attackedAI only knows the world from data humans collected. Fresh, local, weird reality is invisible to it.
Deliverable · A cleaned, labeled, human-verified dataset (first entry in each camper's portfolio)
Project block (self-directed, 45 min) · Finish tagging the team's share of the 150-photo dataset; write one paragraph, "what the model missed and why." Answer key: the common misses are backlit leaves and damaged plants; the poster has the checklist.
Day 3 · Wednesday

The Verification Bureau

0:00-0:10Claim intake. Each team gets a stack of 5 AI-generated "facts" about the town (population, founding year, what's at Main and 3rd). Campers rank their confidence on the claim sheet and write one line: "how I'd check it."
0:10-0:40Verification sprint. Single-group walk to two scripted stops (a shop owner briefed the week before, a public plaque). Measuring tape out, questions asked, photos taken. Every team stays inside the group; the instructor runs the clock, eight minutes per team per stop.
0:40-0:55Verdicts. Back inside. Teams fill verdict cards: verified, busted, or unverifiable, with evidence attached (photo, measurement, primary source). The instructor demos citing a primary source in one minute; each team reads one verdict aloud. The phrase of the day: "we couldn't confirm it."
0:55-1:00Handoff. Remaining-claims handout (finish with a parent or in the project block). Crew collects tapes and clipboards.
Materials, 12 campers, pre-kitted
  • 3 claim stacks x 5 cards (all claims pre-checked by the instructor against primary sources during prep)
  • 12 verdict cards + 3 measuring tapes + 3 field clipboards
  • 1 camera per team; shop-owner briefing sheet (instructor folder)
Prep checklist, one person, about 30 min
  • Pre-verify all 15 claims against primary sources; print the cards
  • Brief the shop owner on the visit window (done the week before)
  • Set the two-stop walking route; print the route card
  • Charge cameras; test the measuring tapes
The weakness attackedAI states invented specifics with total confidence and cannot check a single one.
Deliverable · Evidence-backed verification report on 10 AI claims, at least 3 field-measured
Project block (self-directed, 45 min) · Write the verdict report on the template: claim, verdict, evidence, source line. Answer key is the instructor's pre-verification sheet; it stays in the instructor folder.
Day 4 · Thursday

Sensors and Signal

0:00-0:10Demo. The instructor holds up the finished station, names every part, and sets the safety gate: the solder station runs only while the instructor stands at it. Two pairs at a time; everyone else wires sensors or writes code.
0:10-0:40Build. Pairs rotate through the single heated station (about 6 minutes each) while the rest assemble sensor boards and write the logging program on laptops. The instructor runs the solder station personally, the full 30 minutes, with ventilation and PPE. Juniors follow the wiring-diagram card; seniors calibrate and add a second sensor channel.
0:40-0:55Deploy + compare. The whole group places stations around the venue and yard, inside the instructor's line of sight the entire time. First readings go on the log sheet. Compare them against the printed AI "forecast" from the morning, and discuss: why is fresh measured data worth more than a fluent prediction?
0:55-1:00Handoff. Overnight logging charts issued. Friday's opening is harvest-and-graph, ten minutes. Crew stores the solder gear, locked.
Materials, 12 campers, pre-kitted
  • 12 micro:bit sensor kits (temp, light, sound; USB rechargeable)
  • 1 soldering station + fume extractor + safety glasses
  • 12 wiring-diagram cards; logging program pre-loaded on laptops (USB fallback)
  • 1 printed AI forecast sheet; 12 overnight log charts
Prep checklist, one person, about 25 min
  • Charge the 12 micro:bits; lay out the kits
  • Set up the solder station, test the iron, check the extractor filter
  • Verify the laptop USB drivers; print the forecast sheet
The weakness attackedAI has no senses. It can't take today's temperature, and it can't be trusted to guess it.
Deliverable · Working sensor station + overnight logged dataset, harvested and graphed at the Friday opening
Project block (self-directed, 45 min) · Write five sentences: "why fresh measured data beats a fluent prediction." Hand-graph the first hour of readings. Seniors compute the forecast error; juniors mark the high and low.
Day 5 · Friday

Fabrication Friday

0:00-0:10The challenge. Show the AI-designed phone stand, prompted and printed earlier in the week by the instructor. The rule of the day: no edits to the AI's file. It printed exactly as prompted.
0:10-0:40Test lab. Teams stress-test the prints: drop test, phone fit test, 500g load test. Log every failure on the failure sheet with a cause guess: tolerance, thin wall, or fantasy geometry (a feature that cannot exist in the real world).
0:40-0:55Redesign. Fix exactly one failure in CAD. Juniors use guided templates (thicken the wall, add a fillet); seniors model freeform. The instructor queues the fixed files to print during the project block.
0:55-1:00Handoff. Before-and-after portfolio pages issued. Crew bags the failed prints for the museum shelf.
Materials, 12 campers, pre-kitted
  • 12 pre-printed AI designs (printed Thursday by the instructor, overnight batch)
  • 12 failure log sheets; 12 CAD templates (juniors) + CAD access (seniors)
  • Test rig: 500g weight, drop mat, 3 phone dummies
  • Before-and-after portfolio pages
Prep checklist, one person, about 30 min
  • Print the 12 AI designs in the Thursday overnight batch; check for jams
  • Lay out the test rig; open the CAD templates on the laptops
  • Clear the failed-print museum shelf for Friday's additions
The weakness attackedAI describes objects it has never held. Physics doesn't read the prompt.
Deliverable · Failed AI print + fixed human revision, presented side by side
Project block (self-directed, 45 min) · CAD finishing and print monitoring; mount the before-and-after photo pair with captions: "the AI did this; I fixed that."

Day 6 · Monday

The Interview Room

0:00-0:10Open. AI Fail of the Day, then the mission brief: "Today you learn the question a machine can never ask." Question-craft sheets issued; the visiting guest is introduced, a community member briefed in advance, present all session, never alone with a camper.
0:10-0:35Question craft. Campers draft 8 interview questions on the sheet, then test them twice: once with the AI chatbot on a laptop, once with the guest. The class compares what a real human actually said versus what the chatbot invented. Juniors write fact-finding questions; seniors write questions that earn a story.
0:35-0:55Recording rotation. Six pairs. Three record 8-minute interviews in the interview corners with the field-kit recorders while three transcribe on laptops, then swap. The instructor circulates the whole time; all recording happens inside the room.
0:55-1:00Handoff. "Moment Marker" transcription handout issued. Crew collects the recorders for charging.
Materials, 12 campers, pre-kitted
  • 3 field kits (recorder + lav mic), charged
  • 12 question-craft sheets + 12 "Moment Marker" transcription cards
  • 1 pre-briefed community guest (visiting all session)
  • Laptops with chatbot tab open, content filter on
Prep checklist, one person, about 25 min
  • Confirm the guest's arrival window (briefed the week before)
  • Charge the 3 recorders; test the lav mics
  • Print the question-craft sheets and transcription cards
  • Set 3 interview corners; open the chatbot tab on laptops
The weakness attackedAI's "empathy" is pattern-matched text. Trust is built in person, in a pause, with eye contact.
Deliverable · Recorded 8-minute oral-history interview + marked transcript
Project block (self-directed, 45 min) · Handout: "Moment Marker". Transcribe the interview, mark the hesitations, the laughter, the follow-up that earned the story. Write one paragraph: the moment the machine could never have gotten.
Day 7 · Tuesday

Robotics Gauntlet

0:00-0:10Open. The AI's predicted route for the course, printed and posted. The mission: beat the prediction with a real robot. Course rules, stopwatch demo, safety check on the shared kits.
0:10-0:40Program the wanderer. Teams of 4 share one rover kit (3 kits total). Each team codes, runs a trial, and debugs in rotation; the course never sits idle. When a rover flips or gets lost, the team logs the physical cause: wheel slip, sensor drift, tape that reflects wrong. Seniors add the autonomous waypoint extension.
0:40-0:55The gauntlet. Timed trials, two runs per team, the instructor times and calls the scores. Every run ends with a 60-second debug huddle: what failed in the real world that the simulation never saw?
0:55-1:00Handoff. "Crash Report" log sheet issued. Crew packs the course markers.
Materials, 12 campers, pre-kitted

Prep checklist, one person, about 25 min
  • Charge the 3 rovers; test every sensor
  • Build the course (ramps, tunnel, swamp); tape the lane lines
  • Print the AI route prediction; print the debug log sheets
The weakness attackedAI reasons about space from text. It has never turned a corner, slipped on cardboard, or recovered from a flip.
Deliverable · Gauntlet completion time + debug log of every real-world fix
Project block (self-directed, 45 min) · Handout: "Crash Report". For each failure, the physical cause and the fix, in the team's own words. Seniors sketch the autonomous waypoint plan for Day 9.

04 · A day at camp

The 60-minute lesson: one adult, twelve kids, zero dead time.

There is no 9-to-4 day and no AM/PM split. There is one 60-minute lesson with the instructor, a 45-minute self-directed project block that needs no adult, and 30 minutes of solo prep before the lesson. This is the whole schedule, and it is what makes one instructor enough.

-0:30Instructor prepOpen the day's kit box, lay out twelve stations, run the tool-safety check, charge shared devices. The checklist is printed; nothing is shopped for or improvised.
0:00OpenAI Fail of the Day (5 min), today's mission brief, crew role assignments (materials captain, Wall of Almost curator, reset lead)
0:10CoreOne activity; the instructor circulates for the full 30 minutes. Tool stations run only with the instructor standing at them.
0:40CloseDebrief, evidence logged, project-block handout issued, tomorrow previewed
0:55Pickup transitionHandouts in backpacks, crew resets stations, attendance pinged to parents via Twilio
+0:45Project block (self-directed)Campers work the printed handout in the project corner or at home. No adult instruction needed; the handout, the answer key, and the wall rubric carry the weight.

Safety: one background-checked, first-aid-certified instructor supervises all 12 campers every minute of every session. Tool use (soldering, cutting, printers) is demo-gated at supervised stations; field missions move as a single group, never in buddy splits. Camp is nut-aware; all medications are held by the instructor and logged. (The ten run-of-show plans in §03 are the full curriculum installment; the complete one-adult safety plan lands with rubric item 4 in the coming runs.)

05 · Capstone

Every camper leaves with proof they did what the machine couldn't.

The capstone isn't a diorama, it's a small, real production with an AI in the crew and a kid in charge. Teams of four pick one track and ship it at Showcase:

Track A · The Field Guide

Verified local dataset

A human-collected, human-verified dataset of the neighborhood (species, sounds, measurements), with a model graded against it and every miss explained.

Track B · The Object

Fabricated thing

A physical product born from an AI design, rescued by human engineering: original prompt, failed print, fixed revision, load test on video.

Track C · The Story

Human-sourced piece

An oral-history audio piece no model could record, a real interview, edited, fact-checked, and published with a producer's credit roll.

Every capstone ships with the same final artifact: the credit roll, one page naming what the AI did, what the humans did, what failed, and who fixed it. Parents don't just see the project; they see their kid taking responsibility for a machine's work. That page is the diploma.

06 · Staff

One instructor, by design.

The whole program is engineered so a single adult can run it: twelve campers, sixty minutes a day, every material pre-kitted, every post-lesson project block self-directed. There is no assistant, no CIT, no second pair of eyes, which is exactly why the format, the tool gating, and the admin automation are built the way they are. The instructor is either the founder operating the camp or one hired educator; either way the AB5 classification question is settled, not dodged.

Age-differentiation model (10–14 range): The four-year spread within the cohort is bridged through tiered challenge variants, not separate tracks. Each day's mission ships with a base version (accessible at 10) and an extension layer (expected at 13–14): e.g., Day 2's photo dataset uses GPS tagging for juniors and adds metadata schema design for seniors; Day 5's CAD redesign uses guided templates for 10-year-olds and freeform modeling for 14-year-olds; Day 7's rover gauntlet adds autonomous waypoint navigation for the older cohort. Rotating crew roles give the 13–14s leadership jobs (materials captain, Wall of Almost curator) performed inside the group, in the instructor's line of sight, never as supervision. Kids who already code get harder variants of every mission, the rubric scales, the thesis doesn't.

The instructor · 1 adult

A generalist maker-educator

One person: teaching experience plus hands-on maker fluency, background-checked, first-aid certified, AB 506 trained. Must be able to run a sensor build, a CAD session, a taste panel, and an interview drill in the same week. Pay: $2,000 per two-week session (10 instruction hours plus about 5 hours of prep, roughly $133/hr blended). If hired rather than founder-operated, the instructor is a W-2 employee on Gusto payroll with California workers' comp bound before Day 1. Contractor arrangements are off the table: under California's ABC test (AB5, Labor Code §2775) an on-site, on-schedule instructor delivering the camp's core service is an employee, full stop.

Camper crew · rotating, ages 13–14

Responsibility, not supervision

Two older campers per day take crew jobs with a printed checklist: kit distribution, Wall of Almost curation, end-of-hour reset. These are leadership roles performed inside the group, in the instructor's line of sight at all times. Nobody under 18 ever supervises, and no camper is ever out of the group.

The automation layer

Admin that needs no human

Everything a second adult used to absorb, check-in, parent pings, attendance, pickup authorization records, incident timestamps, is handled by the API stack in §10 (Airtable, Twilio, Typeform). The solo instructor teaches; the software does the paperwork. That is what makes one adult enough.

Prep · 30 minutes max

Kitted the day before

Every day's materials arrive as one pre-packed kit with a 30-minute prep checklist: open the kit, lay out twelve stations, run the tool-safety check, charge the shared devices. No same-day shopping, no improvisation. Each day in §03 carries its own materials list and checklist as the curriculum is converted to 60-minute lesson plans.

10 · Execution plan

The launch plan: automation for the paperwork, humans for the judgment.

This camp can be built by one operator + a stack of real APIs, with humans kept exactly where the law, insurers, and child safety require them: supervising children and owning judgment calls. Below: the full launch plan, seven phases, T-minus timeline, itemized budget, kill gates, and the honest list of steps that cannot be human-free. Every named API was verified to exist in Aug 2026; every marketplace that doesn't expose an API is flagged and given a workaround.

The operating thesis APIs do the paperwork: form the entity, bind insurance, run background checks, push ads, collect tuition, text parents, track rosters. Humans own the judgment: permit walk-throughs, insurance riders, adjudicating a flagged background check, and, non-negotiably, supervising children. Roughly 90% of the launch tasks run without a human touching them; the remaining 10% is the part that makes it legal and safe, compressed into seven single-decision human gates.

Phase 1 · T-20 → T-16 weeks (Jan–Feb)

Compliance & insurance

~4 weeks · ~$3,450

This plan complies with all three. human seam: county inspector

Honest seams, the humans this plan refuses to remove

Each seam is compressed to a single human decision, made once (or once a day), with everything else prepared by the stack:

  • 1 · County health inspector, the organized-camp permit requires a human walk-through and sign-off at the venue. One meeting, T-2 weeks.
  • 2 · Insurance broker, the abuse & molestation endorsement isn't self-serve on any API. One call, T-20 weeks.
  • 3 · Background-check adjudication, FCRA adverse-action review of any flagged candidate. A human reads, a human decides, per candidate.
  • 4 · Venue deposit approval, one decision on a shortlist of six. Money moves only after the click.
  • 5 · The adult in the room, one trained, background-checked, first-aid-certified adult on site every single minute campers are present, backed by the automated admin stack (Airtable, Twilio, Typeform). The 1:12 cap is a hard ceiling, and the 60-minute lesson format with self-directed project blocks is what makes one adult sufficient. This isn't overhead; it's the product.
  • 6 · Medication & incident command, the director administers meds and owns every incident call. Never automatable, never delegated to software.
  • 7 · Pickup authorization, photo-ID match against the signed authorization record, at the door, every day. The robot never dismisses a child.

11 · FAQ

The questions parents actually ask.

Does my kid need AI or coding experience?

None. We assume zero on Day 1 and teach every tool from the button up. The camp's core skills, observing, checking, building, asking, deciding, need no prerequisites. Kids who already code get harder variants of every mission.

Wait, is this two weeks of screens?

The opposite. Roughly half of every day is outside or at a workbench: walking the block with a camera, soldering a sensor, testing prints, flying rovers through an obstacle course. AI tools appear constantly, but as the thing being directed, checked, and out-performed, not the destination.

Soldering irons and 3D printers at age 10?

Every tool is introduced with a supervised demonstration and a safety gate a camper must pass before solo use, always within arm's reach of the instructor. Heated tools run at supervised stations with ventilation and PPE. With one adult and twelve campers, tool use is station-gated: only one heated station runs at a time, the rest of the group works on non-tool tasks. Our instructor owns tool safety personally.

Which AI tools do you use, and are they safe for kids?

School-managed accounts on mainstream, kid-safe AI tools (image generation, chat, CAD assistants) with content filtering on, no personal data entered, and strict "we don't feed it our faces or friends' names" rules taught on Day 2. Accounts are camp-owned and wiped at session end.

What does my kid actually take home?

A producer's portfolio: their verified dataset, the failed print and its human-fixed twin, sensor graphs, interview audio, the debugging log, and the signed credit roll, plus a diploma reading Producer of AI Projects, Certified Human.

Is there financial aid?

Yes, a donor-funded scholarship bench covers 2 of 12 seats per session, prioritized through local school partners. Application is one short form; decisions are need-blind and made before enrollment closes.

Why "producers" and not "prompt engineers"?

Prompting is a technique; producing is a job. A producer owns the outcome: the data, the checks, the fixes, and the blame when it's wrong. Prompting is one arrow in that quiver, we teach all ten.

What if AI gets better at all this by 2027?

Then the curriculum sharpens, the weakness list is re-audited each spring and days are rebuilt around whatever AI is currently worst at. The mission outlives any specific failure mode: the human who verifies, builds, and takes responsibility stays essential. (Also: we'll believe the demo when we can check it. That's the whole camp.)
